Since graduating two years ago, I have always been the youngest in the room. "Wow, you're so young" is a common verbatim I hear when I tell people my age.
 
But nobody told me that being the youngest in the room comes with its own strange curriculum, the one you have to figure out mostly by getting things wrong. So here's what I wish someone had told me on day one.
 
## The Unspoken Tax on Being Inexperienced
 
When you're the youngest, you pay a tax that nobody announces. It shows up as:
 
**The knowledge gap.** You're in meetings where people throw around acronyms and context you've never encountered. You nod anyway. And then you get scolded for pretending that you know something.
 
**Imposter syndrome.** Not the aesthetic kind people post about on LinkedIn. The real kind where someone asks you a question and your mind goes completely blank, and you can hear your own heartbeat.
 
**The overwhelm of other people's overwhelm.** When a stressful-looking manager comes to you with a half-formed problem and frantic energy, and you have to stay calm while internally you're also panicking.
 
**Not knowing what you don't know.** This is the sneakiest one. You can't search up the internet for what you've never heard of.
 
My coping mechanisms were... not great. I kept a blank, serious expression at all times. I stayed late at the office. I didn't talk to people much. I told myself it was because I'm an introvert. That was only half true.
 
## The Moment That Changed How I Thought About This
 
Here's the uncomfortable truth that took me way too long to accept:
 
People already know you're the least experienced person in the room.
 
You're not fooling anyone by looking stoic or staying late. And here's the bigger shocker: they're often not coming to you expecting you to solve the problem. They might be getting a second opinion. They might be assessing how you think. They might just need to talk it through with someone.
 
A senior gave me a tip that shifted everything: **Don't immediately solve the problem. Walk them through it.**
 
Ask the 5W1Hs. Get a pen and paper if you have to. Draw it out. Talk out your steps. Show your working, not just your answer. And always, always leave room to be wrong: "I'm happy to change my view if there's something I'm missing."
 
That one habit, thinking out loud instead of trying to appear like you have all the answers, did more for my credibility than any late night at the office ever did.
 
## Stop Asking "What Should I Build?" Start Asking "What Does This System Need?"
 
A useful shift in thinking is to stop rushing straight into execution and spend more time understanding the system first.
 
Not doing nothing, but mapping. Understanding the workflow. The structure. The way things actually move, not just the way they are supposed to move on paper.
 
Most people jump in asking: "What tasks do I have this week?"
 
A better question is: "What is the current bottleneck, and is there something I can do to remove it?"
 
Most people ask: "What should I build?"
 
Try asking instead: "What does this system need?"
 
They sound similar, but they lead to very different places.
 
This applies beyond work, too. When there's a problem, the best move is not always to rush into solutions. It's to find the bottleneck: the one thing that, if removed, makes everything else flow better.
 
Then focus there.
 
It's a simple framework, but it cuts through a lot of noise.
 
## Building Credibility Is a Slow Game. Play It Anyway.
 
Here's the timeline nobody gives you: it takes a minimum of six months in any new team before you start to feel established. Six months before people begin to trust your judgment by default, not just case by case.
 
That sounds demoralising. I think it's actually freeing.
 
Because it means the pressure to prove yourself in one month is largely self-imposed. What actually builds credibility is simpler:
 
- **Deliver small things well.** Not flashy things. Small, assigned things done thoroughly, on time, with detail.
- **Track the rework.** How often are people following up on your deliverables with questions? How often does something you handed over need to come back? That's your real feedback loop.
- **Take up space slowly.** Ask more questions in meetings. Not to perform curiosity but to actually understand.
I started doing end-to-end ownership of small problems. That meant I understood every step, not just my slice. It meant when questions came, I had answers.
 
## Shadow Like It's Your Job (Because It Is)
 
Ask your seniors to bring you to meetings. Not to contribute, but just to observe.
 
Watch how they handle conflict. Do they get defensive when challenged, or do they slow down and try to understand where the other person is coming from? Watch how they frame problems. Watch how they disagree without making it personal.
 
This is free mentorship that most people leave on the table because they're afraid of asking.
 
## Keep a Brag Sheet (But Not For the Reason You Think)
 
I keep a running document of things I've done, problems I've identified, solutions I've tested, and crucially how I measured whether those solutions worked.
 
Not to show anyone. Not to impress a manager in a review.
 
It's for me. Because when something gets thrown at me and my brain goes into panic mode, I can open that document and ask: have I been here before? What did I do then? Did it work?
 
Most of the time, I have been there before. I just forgot. The brag sheet is a memory upgrade.
 
## The Double-Edged Sword
 
Being the youngest in the room is genuinely hard. People will underestimate you: sometimes dismissively, sometimes condescendingly, sometimes without even realising they're doing it.
 
But that underestimation is also a kind of cover. When you deliver something excellent, the contrast is striking. Nobody sees it coming. And that moment when the room recalibrates is worth every late night, every stoic face you practised in the mirror, every question you were too scared to ask but eventually did.
 
The game isn't to pretend you belong. The game is to do the work until you do.
 
If you're the youngest on your team right now, I hope this helped. The road is slower than you want it to be but it's more navigable than it looks.
